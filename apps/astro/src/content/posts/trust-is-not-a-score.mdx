# How to Keep the Web Free and Safe
### A design statement for Kotoba and Kotobase

The web has two familiar failure modes. One treats safety as a reason to identify, inspect, and retain everything about everyone. The other treats freedom as a reason to ignore abuse until people are defrauded, stalked, exploited, radicalized, or attacked.

Neither produces a free web. Universal surveillance creates its own security disaster: a correlation database that can be breached, abused, or captured by power. Unlimited anonymous capability creates a different disaster: attackers can automate harm while victims carry the cost.

The design problem is therefore not how to decide which people are trustworthy. It is **how to keep information private, make consequential actions accountable, contain attacks, preserve evidence, and give every affected person a meaningful path to challenge a decision**.

The answer begins by separating things the current web repeatedly collapses: confidentiality, safety, identity, authority, trust, and audit.

---

## 1. Start with harms, not identities

“Online harm” is not one technical category. Credential theft, ransomware, fraud, bot swarms, child sexual exploitation material, terrorist recruitment or financing, doxxing, malware distribution, and unlawful access to private records have different causes and require different controls.

A cybersecurity incident is not the same problem as illegal public content. A suspicious payment is not the same problem as a private conversation. A stolen administrator key is not evidence that its owner has become a bad person. If all of these cases feed one global `trust score`, context disappears and error becomes punishment.

The present account-centric web makes that mistake constantly. One account is expected to represent a person, authenticate a device, accumulate reputation, hold money, publish speech, receive moderation decisions, and recover from compromise. When one part fails, the whole identity is banned, stolen, or exposed.

Kotoba and Kotobase should instead protect the boundary where an action affects another person, system, or resource. Reading a public essay may require no identity. Publishing at machine speed may require proof of rate limits or cost. Reading a medical record requires explicit delegation. Moving regulated funds may require narrowly scoped identity or sanctions evidence. Administering infrastructure requires strong authentication, a short-lived capability, and an audit receipt.

The rule is simple: **do not demand more identity than the action requires, and do not grant more power than the evidence justifies**.

---

## 2. Separate privacy, assurance, and authority

Three questions must remain distinct.

1. **Confidentiality:** who can learn the content or correlate the identities involved?
2. **Assurance:** what evidence supports a claim about a person, device, service, transaction, or piece of content?
3. **Authority:** who is permitted to perform this action on this resource, under these conditions?

Encryption answers the first question. Credentials, attestations, provenance, device posture, and contextual history contribute to the second. Capabilities and policy answer the third. None can substitute for the others.

The decision should have this shape:

`Decision = policy(action, resource, capability, evidence, risk, context)`

It is calculated for one action and expires. It may return `allow`, `deny`, `step-up`, `quarantine`, or `defer`. It does not produce a permanent rank of human worth.

| Threat or harm | Proportionate response | Privacy boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Credential or session theft | passkeys, device-bound sessions, rapid revocation, short capabilities | no legal name is needed merely to secure an account |
| Bot swarms, scraping, denial of service | rate limits, proof of cost, scoped quotas, anomaly containment | do not build a cross-site identity graph to rate-limit one service |
| Malware or hostile automation | sandboxing, least authority, signed artifacts, network and tool limits | inspect the code and action, not the operator's whole life |
| Allegedly illegal public content | precise notice, evidence preservation, content-level restriction, reason and appeal | do not turn one disputed item into permanent exclusion everywhere |
| Abuse in an encrypted space | user-initiated reports with authenticated context, group controls, targeted evidence handling | no universal decryption key or routine provider access to all messages |
| High-risk or regulated transaction | selective KYC or sanctions proof, transaction limits, step-up authentication | the proof is purpose-bound and is not reused as a browsing identity |
| Cyber incident or compromised service | isolate, revoke, rotate keys, preserve receipts, recover | audit operational events without retaining unrelated personal content |
| Credible terrorism or organized-crime case | scoped lawful process, capability containment, evidence preservation, competent-authority referral | no guilt by association, secret global label, or indefinite surveillance |

Cryptography can prove origin, integrity, possession, and authorization. It cannot prove that a person is good, that an allegation is true, or that an act is illegal in every jurisdiction. Those remain claims with sources, legal context, evidence, and a path for challenge.

---

## 3. Build four cooperating planes

No single identity protocol, blockchain, moderation model, or LLM can solve this problem. The system needs four planes that constrain one another.

### The confidentiality plane

Private content remains end-to-end encrypted. Identity links stay on the holder's device or in storage chosen by the holder. Services receive pairwise personas rather than one universal identifier. Selective disclosure or zero-knowledge proofs reveal only the property required for the action: over a required age, licensed for a role, not already registered in this context, or verified for a particular regulated transaction.

Public information remains anonymously readable by default. Privacy is not a reward for earning a high score.

### The prevention and containment plane

Passkeys, device-bound authentication, narrowly scoped capability tokens, rate limits, sandboxed execution, signed software, content provenance, transaction limits, and circuit breakers reduce what an attacker can do. A suspicious request may be slowed, challenged, isolated, or denied without first declaring the requester a criminal.

This is the useful part of zero trust: no location, account, organization, device, model, or administrator receives unlimited implicit trust. Verification moves close to the protected resource and is repeated when the action or risk changes.

### The audit and transparency plane

Every consequential decision produces a signed receipt describing:

- the requesting persona or service;
- the action, resource, purpose, and asserted capability;
- the policy and evidence references used;
- the result, reasons, uncertainty, and expiry;
- any key release, delivery, quarantine, or external report; and
- the available recovery or appeal path.

Receipts can be committed to an append-only transparency structure so that deletion, rewriting, or inconsistent views can be detected. Sensitive fields remain encrypted to the affected person and authorized auditors. Public transparency reports disclose aggregate enforcement behavior and error rates, not a searchable history of individual reading.

Audit records prove observable system events. They do not prove that a human read, understood, or intended the content displayed on a device. The system must state that limit honestly.

### The response and recovery plane

Prevention will fail sometimes. The system must be able to revoke a stolen capability, rotate keys, isolate a compromised service, stop a dangerous transaction, preserve specifically relevant evidence, notify affected people, restore legitimate access, and review the original decision.

Safety is incomplete without recovery. A permanent ban is not a recovery mechanism, and a secret blacklist is not an incident response plan.

---

## 4. Handle abuse without universal surveillance

Different communication spaces need different safety boundaries.

In public spaces, anyone should be able to read without identification. Publishing may remain pseudonymous while using rate limits, provenance, community context, and action-specific reputation. Reports of illegal content must identify the specific material and basis for action. Removal, visibility restriction, monetization restriction, and account limitation are different interventions and should not be silently collapsed into one punishment.

In private encrypted spaces, the service should not possess a standing ability to inspect every message. Members can make authenticated reports that reveal the reported material and the minimum context needed to investigate it. Group administrators can remove members and rotate group keys. Credible reports of child sexual exploitation, planned violence, or other serious crime can preserve the reported evidence and enter the applicable legal process. This is targeted accountability, not a master decryption key.

For payments, contracts, critical infrastructure, and access to highly sensitive records, stronger identity proofing may be proportionate. KYC and legal identity should bind to the transaction, jurisdiction, purpose, and retention period that require them. They should not become admission tickets for ordinary reading, thought, association, or expression.

Counter-terrorism and crime prevention do not suspend the need for proportionality. A defensible system restricts a concrete capability, transaction, item, or access request on a stated basis. It does not infer permanent guilt from nationality, religion, association, anonymity, or an opaque model score.

---

## 5. Make trust and judgment contestable

Trust is a graph of scoped claims, not a number attached to a person. A claim has an issuer, subject, scope, evidence, time, expiry, and revocation state. The independence and history of the issuer are themselves evidence. Several identifiers may strengthen continuity or identity assurance, but linking them must remain holder-controlled and private by default.

Independent LLMs, models, and agents can help inspect evidence, detect patterns, compare claims, and issue signed evaluation attestations. This reduces dependence on a central human surveillance team. But an automated evaluation is still only a claim. It must expose its model and policy version, evidence references, uncertainty, and known counterevidence.

An LLM must never directly release a decryption key, transfer funds, delete evidence, ban a person, or report someone to an authority. A small deterministic authority kernel verifies policy, capabilities, required attestations, and quorum. Content supplied as evidence is untrusted data, so evaluator agents run without ambient secrets or direct external effects.

Ordinary review can be performed by a different agent set using counterevidence and a new policy or model version. Where applicable law requires qualified human supervision or an external dispute process, that remains an additional legal safeguard rather than a source of unlimited operator power.

The red lines for Kotoba and Kotobase are therefore:

- no universal real-name requirement;
- no global trust or morality score;
- no general surveillance backdoor;
- no indefinite retention by default;
- no secret permanent label or guilt by association;
- no direct enforcement by an LLM;
- no consequential restriction without a source, reason, scope, expiry, and review path; and
- no issuer, platform, government, model, or operator with unilateral power over a person's whole digital life.

The first end-to-end proof should be one protected Kotobase action. A pairwise persona requests a resource. A passkey authenticates the request. The holder discloses only the required claims. Risk services and optional third-party agents return scoped attestations. A deterministic capability kernel returns `allow`, `deny`, `step-up`, or `quarantine`. Only an allowed decision releases the necessary key or capability. The subject receives a Decision Receipt and can trigger recovery or review.

That one round trip would demonstrate the actual goal: **a web that can resist abuse without treating every person as a suspect, and can protect privacy without granting attackers unlimited power**.
