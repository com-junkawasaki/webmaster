Freedom Is Enlightenment — Kanjizai and Root Access to Self-Definition

Jun Kawasaki / 河崎純真 — September 9, 2026

Foundations: Mathematics, Theology, Philosophy, and Ontology.

Freedom is enlightenment: recognizing the non-separation of self and other while having root access to define oneself.

This is how I understand freedom. It includes the ability to reconsider the perspectives and common sense that constitute a self-image, and to define oneself anew, beyond choosing only within an image already given.

For me this question lies at the center of everyday relationships, organizations, and the making of technology. If we are constituted through relation, what relations should we create, change, and undertake?

1. Knowing Means Taking Responsibility for How We Divide the World

When describing the world, we choose what counts as one thing, which differences matter, and which relations to preserve.

Calling someone “trustworthy” can conceal who trusts them, concerning what, until when, and on what grounds. Assigning a single property to a person can hide the conditions of the relationship.

I want descriptions of knowledge to retain the perspective and evidence that made them possible: the speaker, time of observation, scope, counterevidence, and corrections.

This does not surrender truth to preference. Because every description can be mistaken, we must state what could correct it. The strength of knowledge cannot be measured by the force of an assertion alone.

2. The Self Changes While Continuing

The self has a body, habits, and memories. Language, tools, and relationships also change its behavior and experience.

I have approached this as a deformation of self-boundaries. The information-geometric model in my earlier essay is one proposal for investigating that question quantitatively. Spirit Is Information

But a model of a person must not be mistaken for the whole person. Similar records of states do not establish that the same life was lived.

I am not identical to yesterday’s self in every respect. Nor have I become unrelated to that self. Both continuity and change must be addressed.

3. Freedom Is Root Access to Self-Definition

We receive many definitions before we choose them: names, family roles, social positions, and standards of success or failure. They enter the image expressed by “this is the kind of person I am.”

If we can choose only within those definitions, our freedom remains inside an assigned permission set. Enlightenment recognizes that we have treated the definitions as absolute and returns us to their configuration. I call this root access. Enlightenment Is Root Access

What do I count as self? How far does my responsibility extend? By which values and roles will I live? The conscious ability to redefine these is freedom.

Self-definition here belongs together with non-separation. An isolated, unchanging ego does not acquire ownership of the whole. A self arising within relation takes up its boundaries and way of living anew. The activity of redefining itself also arises through a body, language, and encounters with others.

4. Kanjizai: Seeing Freely

The figure I find especially important here is Kanjizai Bodhisattva, who appears at the beginning of the Heart Sutra.

The Heart Sutra is a short scripture widely recited in Japan. My impression is that many people two generations older than me know it by heart. This expresses my sense of its familiarity rather than the result of a generational survey. Its place in Japanese recitation

Kanjizai Bodhisattva is the same bodhisattva as Kanzeon, or Kannon: Avalokiteshvara. Kanjizai is not a separate, lower-ranking figure. Nor does “bodhisattva” mean merely a believer in the Buddha. It concerns a practitioner of awakening and the liberation of sentient beings. Daikakuji’s account

My attention is drawn to the figure who practices. At the beginning of the scripture stands one who sees. I receive this widely loved bodhisattva through that active dimension, beyond obedience to a distant authority alone.

Kanjizai: seeing freely.

I read the name as the ability to examine the standpoint itself instead of remaining confined to one prescribed perspective. The connection from this reading to root access is my own philosophical interpretation.

5. Form Is Emptiness; Emptiness Is Form: Releasing Fixed Definitions

“Form is emptiness” loosens the grasp that treats appearances as fixed substances. “Emptiness is form” returns us to concrete appearances: releasing that grasp does not make the world disappear.

In my reading, undoing perspectives and common sense means undoing their claim to absoluteness. “I can only be this role.” “The world is exhausted by this classification.” We release these fixations one by one, without turning emptiness into a new object of attachment.

The Heart Sutra extends its negations beyond sensation and cognition to suffering, its origin, cessation, and the path, and even to wisdom and attainment. The teaching intended to free us must not become another unchangeable self-image. I read that thoroughness in the text.

The Kanjizai that appears through this practice is not a newly discovered substance. The activity of seeing, previously bound to a fixed standpoint, becomes free. This describes how the practice opens to us, not the sequence of a character’s appearance in the scripture.

Because things are empty, definitions are not absolute. Because things appear as form, we can define concretely and live. To define oneself without becoming imprisoned by that definition: this is what I call enlightenment and freedom.

6. Exercising Freedom within Relation

Root access is a metaphor for returning to the definition of oneself. It does not mean that physical laws or past events change merely because one wills it.

The freedom to redefine oneself does not confer a right to define another unilaterally. Others also have the freedom of self-definition and refusal. Understanding non-separation does not make their consent unnecessary.

The ability to reorganize relations is therefore the practical expression of freedom as enlightenment: choosing roles anew, questioning common sense, rebuilding relationships, and accepting what those choices bring to others.

7. Can Love Preserve the Other’s Difference?

I understand love as self-expansion. But expanding the range of the self and relating well are not identical. Love as Self-Expansion

Thinking of someone as “part of me” can obscure their will. Families, organizations, religions, and states can all encounter this problem.

Good relationships contain both connection and boundaries. They distinguish what we decide together from what each decides alone. They leave room for what we do not understand and receive refusal. Non-separation cannot be a reason to omit consent.

I ask about the life a relationship makes possible for another, as well as the breadth of its love.

8. Responsibility Becomes Concrete along Relations

A relational self does not mean that everything can be excused as the environment’s fault. We must ask what an actor knew, could control, and undertook to do.

Responsibility includes institutional structures and organizational design as well as individuals. Many participants must not make each participant’s actions disappear.

At the same time, I do not want to fix a person to a single failure. Later conduct can change even when a record remains. Responsibility for the past and possibilities for the future must both survive. I have described this as a design principle for trust and return. Preserving history while permitting return

9. Technology Makes Philosophical Choices Operative

What should be preserved? What counts as one subject? Who may change something? How can a mistaken judgment be corrected? These are software specifications and choices about people and the world.

What connects Kotoba and Kotobase to my philosophy is the attempt to work with relations and history, enable correction, and support inheritance. Whether that direction actually helps people live must be tested through implementation and operation. An earlier substrate design

I seek a life in which we can be free without isolation, change without erasing the past, and connect without owning one another.

The Heart Sutra in Full

The following is a new English rendering of the classical Chinese text attributed to Xuanzang. It includes the concluding title. It is not a quotation from a modern English translation. The interpretive discussion above is my own; the text below provides the scripture to which it responds. Classical source

The Heart Sutra of the Perfection of Wisdom

Avalokiteshvara Bodhisattva, while practicing the profound perfection of wisdom, clearly saw that the five aggregates were all empty, and crossed beyond all suffering and distress.

Shariputra, form does not differ from emptiness; emptiness does not differ from form. Form is precisely emptiness; emptiness is precisely form. Feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are also like this.

Shariputra, all phenomena bear the mark of emptiness: neither arising nor ceasing, neither defiled nor pure, neither increasing nor decreasing.

Therefore, in emptiness there is no form, no feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness; no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind; no form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or object of mind; no realm of the eye, and so on through no realm of mental consciousness.

There is no ignorance and no ending of ignorance, and so on through no aging and death and no ending of aging and death. There is no suffering, origin, cessation, or path; no wisdom and no attainment, because there is nothing to attain.

Bodhisattvas, relying on the perfection of wisdom, have no obstruction in their minds. Having no obstruction, they have no fear. Far removed from inverted views and dreamlike imaginings, they reach ultimate nirvana.

The buddhas of the three times, relying on the perfection of wisdom, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening.

Therefore know the perfection of wisdom to be the great sacred mantra, the mantra of great illumination, the unsurpassed mantra, the unequalled mantra, able to remove all suffering, true and not false.

Therefore the mantra of the perfection of wisdom is proclaimed. The mantra is spoken thus:

Gate gate paragate parasamgate bodhi svaha.

The Heart Sutra of the Perfection of Wisdom

The mantra is retained in Sanskrit transliteration, as a mantra, rather than presented as an unambiguous English sentence.
