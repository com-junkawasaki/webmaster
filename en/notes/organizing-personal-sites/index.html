Organizing learning notes on a personal site — 10 examples

2026-11-26 · Personal websites

I want to be able to find a diagram or a comparison again after I have moved on to something else. The main posts have room for developed ideas. A learning note can answer a single question or hold a comparison I expect to revisit. I looked at ten public sites with two questions in mind: where does each kind of writing go, and how do you find it later?

Ten examples

WriterHow the site is organized
Paul GrahamEssays share a title index, with three suggested starting points at the top. A new reader gets a short list even as the archive grows. Essays
Simon WillisonThe Weblog links to a separate TIL (Today I Learned) site with a topic index, search, and its own Atom feed. Weblog · TIL
Martin FowlerThe Bliki gives short or still-forming ideas a place to appear as they arise and accumulate into a collection of linked pieces. What Is a Bliki
Andy MatuschakThe main site highlights selected Working Notes. In the notebook, he publishes notes made for his own use and readers can follow links between them. Main site · Working Notes
Julia EvansThe blog puts the ten latest posts above the full category index. Wizard Zines provides a separate entry point for illustrated booklets. Blog · Wizard Zines
Joel SpolskyThe home page combines recent posts with a Top 10 and reading lists for audiences such as new developers and tech leads. Joel on Software
John GruberThe Linked List gives external links and short commentary their own monthly archive. Those items also appear in the home-page stream. Home · Linked List
Dan LuuTechnical analysis, measurements, and miscellaneous writing share a simple dated title list. Index
Nikita Prokopov (tonsky)Blog, Work, and Talks have their own entrances. The blog lists articles by year. tonsky.me
Brendan GreggA chronological blog sits alongside subject pages such as Flame Graphs and Linux Perf, where readers can return to instructions and examples. Home · CPU Flame Graphs

What to borrow

A dedicated entrance like Simon Willison’s makes it easy to keep publishing small discoveries. Giving each question or concept its own page, as Martin Fowler and Andy Matuschak do, lets it grow through revisions and links. When a diagram or procedure is likely to be used repeatedly, Brendan Gregg’s subject pages show the value of a link readers can revisit by topic.

A simple title list in the style of Dan Luu is enough at the beginning. Once readers have enough material to benefit from a path through a topic, Julia Evans’s category listings and Joel Spolsky’s reading lists offer two ways to organize that path. Paul Graham’s short list at the top of Essays is a useful model for introducing the main writing.

The approach adopted on junkawasaki.com

  • Give each kind of writing an entrance. Main articles live under the existing /posts/ routes. Learning notes have their own Japanese and English indexes on the same site, with a small link from the home page.
  • Give each question a stable URL. Both languages share a slug. The index shows a date and a topic for each note. Start with the question, write down what you found, and add diagrams, comparisons, and primary sources as needed.
  • Update the page when the question stays the same. Fix a diagram or add a reference in place. When a new question can stand on its own, give it a page and link the two. The 11 storage structures note serves as an overview. A measured comparison of B+ tree and LSM-tree writes would warrant its own note.
  • Use a main post for a developed argument. When several notes support an argument or a set of results worth presenting to a reader, write that post and cite the notes it draws on.

The current index is chronological and shows each note’s topic. A topic index becomes useful once readers regularly want to browse a subject. Add a short reading list when the order matters, and consider search when the index makes it hard to find a known note.
