Consciousness Is Computation — Experience Becoming, and Escher's Staircase

Jun Kawasaki — November 17, 2026

This essay explores a hypothesis before any substantial experimental or theoretical validation. Its title states a proposition to consider, rather than an established scientific conclusion.

Why Can It Look That Way Even When We Know?

In an Escher print, a staircase can keep rising and still return to its starting point. Follow it step by step and it goes up. Complete the circuit and you are back where you began.

We recognize the impossibility. Yet recognizing it need not dissolve the local impression of ascending.

That discrepancy interests me. If we know what is wrong, why does the appearance not simply correct itself? Why do knowing and seeing fail to coincide?

It suggests a possibility: the world we consciously experience might be something assembled through computation, rather than the external world received without transformation.

Reading the Parts and Making the Whole Coherent

The print is M. C. Escher’s Ascending and Descending (1960). The Escher museum’s account describes its background and its connection to the Penrose staircase.

What interests me is the difference between reading a local relation and establishing global consistency. A transition from one step to the next can look plausible, while the resulting whole cannot exist as an ordinary three-dimensional staircase.

We proceed as though the next connection will work too. An interpretation formed along the way remains available even after we discover the contradiction. That is one way I want to understand the experience of this print.

This is not an experimental finding that everyone scans the image in the same order. It is an interpretation of processing suggested to me by the work.

Is Consciousness Computation with an Order?

By computation, I mean more than arithmetic or equations. I mean extracting relations from cues, comparing them with prior experience, forming a provisional interpretation, and using that interpretation in further processing.

If appearances arise this way, order matters. An interpretation formed earlier changes how later information is read. Detecting a global contradiction and reading a local step need not reach the same conclusion at the same moment.

An illusion can then be read as an occasion when the construction of an interpretation becomes visible. Procedures that ordinarily yield a plausible world produce a conflict when confronted with an unusual image.

This does not establish that the brain is a serial computer executing one instruction at a time. Processing may run in parallel or return to earlier stages. Here, order primarily means dependency: one process uses something another process has produced. This is not yet a hypothesis specifying particular neural circuits or processing times.

Consciousness Comes into Being Each Time

I want to take one further step.

At first I thought: consciousness is doing the computing. But that sounds as though a conscious self must already be there to assemble the world.

Perhaps the order is reversed. What computation assembles appears as the experience that I am seeing this now. Perhaps we call that appearance consciousness.

At first I wanted to call this something that computation brings to mind. But that can sound like retrieving content already stored somewhere. What I mean is closer to becoming: past experience and present cues are inherited, and present experience itself comes into being anew.

We read the stairs. A relation of ascent forms and appears as the world now seen. Following the image further reveals a contradiction. The next experience contains both “it looks as though it rises” and “this cannot actually work.”

Consciousness might be a process in which interpretations are revised and arise as experience, rather than a place from which a completed picture is watched. On this view, the activity of computing and the content it produces belong to a continuing cycle.

Thinking with Whitehead

This direction is close to Whitehead’s philosophy of process. His concepts of prehension and concrescence in Process and Reality suggest receiving what precedes an occasion and integrating it into a new experience. The subject itself takes shape in that becoming. Relevant passage in the original work, machine-transcribed

This invites us to reconsider the picture of an already completed self waiting to receive information. In the event of taking up relations with the world, the self seeing this way now also comes into being. Perhaps following Escher’s stairs renews both the world’s appearance and the way the experiencing self takes shape.

For Whitehead, however, not all experience is conscious, and prehension is not restricted to conscious cognition. Nor can phases of concrescence simply be mapped onto a timeline of brain processing. I am not attributing a computational theory of consciousness to him. Taking account of his distinction between prehension and consciousness, I want to ask how far computation can describe consciousness as something that comes into being.

This connects with the question of prehension in my earlier Information Is an Organism. Here I leave it as a modest proposal about experience, prior to formalization.

How Far Can an Illusion Take Us?

There is still a substantial leap here.

Saying that perception involves computation differs from saying that consciousness itself is computation. A mechanism that explains an illusion does not, by itself, establish whether that mechanism has subjective experience. We would also need to explain its difference from processing that never becomes conscious.

The contradiction in an Escher print cannot explain every illusion either. Illusions of brightness or motion, errors of memory, and mistaken beliefs need not share one explanation in terms of order.

If computation becomes a name for everything that happens in the mind, it can accommodate every outcome while testing nothing. Developing this hypothesis would require specifying inputs, transformations, and predicted results.

For example, we could compare an impossible staircase with a similar ordinary staircase, vary the order in which parts are shown and the delay before revealing the whole, and ask what first appeared and when a contradiction was noticed. Perhaps earlier exposure would measurably change later interpretation.

This is a possible investigation, not an experiment I have performed. Finding no effect would call for revising this proposal about order. Finding an effect would still require separating attention, memory, and reporting effects; it would not by itself prove that consciousness is computation.

Before an Impossible Staircase

I am drawn to this hypothesis because an illusion makes me question the certainty of seeing.

The self that knows the staircase is impossible and the self that sees it rising belong to the same person. I try thinking of their disagreement as something produced at different stages of computation. Then I try thinking of the experience containing that disagreement as something that continually comes into being, inheriting relations with the world.

Consciousness is computation.

I cannot yet prove those words. But when I follow Escher’s staircase with my eyes, I feel as though I am partway through a procedure for reading the world.

Read one step. Read the next. Then construct, once again, what it was that I was seeing.
